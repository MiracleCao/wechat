Although thermal exposure is a commonly used engineering process to enhance the properties of ti- 
tanium alloys by diffusing oxygen onto the surface, the mechanism by which it affects fatigue perfor- 
mance is not fully understood. This work systematically investigates the effect of thermal exposure on 
the fatigue properties of a near- α titanium alloy through a combination of fatigue testing, in-situ nanoin- 
dentation, micropillar compression experiments, and an integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)- 
scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM), particularly emphasizing the inhibitory role of sur- 
face oxygen-enriched layer on fatigue crack propagation. Results show that after thermal exposure, the 
oxygen-enriched layer formed on the alloy’s surface relieves surface stress concentration, thus enabling 
the alloy to maintain high fatigue strength even after up to 107 fatigue cycles at 650 MPa. Interstitial 
oxygen atoms within the oxygen-enriched layer induce lattice distortion and promote the formation of 
oxygen-enriched stacking faults at fatigue crack tips. These oxygen-enriched stacking faults eﬃciently 
absorb energy required for crack propagation, deﬂect the crack propagation path, extend the propaga- 
tion period, and alleviate stress concentration; these effects collectively enhance the fatigue resistance 
of the alloy. This work systematically elucidates the mechanism by which the oxygen-rich layer delays 
crack propagation through microstructural regulation, providing a theoretical basis for designing high- 
performance titanium alloys.

1. Introduction 
Near- α titanium alloys possess extremely high speciﬁc strength, 
good heat resistance, and excellent creep resistance [ 1–3 ], and they 
are often used as critical hot-end components. Their performance 
stability directly affects the overall reliability and safety of the 
equipment [ 4–6 ]. However, near- α titanium alloys face increasingly 
severe challenges in terms of operating temperature and load- 
bearing capacity. Traditional titanium alloy strengthening methods 
have reached their limit in improving thermal strength [ 7–9 ]; thus, 
new approaches are needed to enhance fatigue performance under 
extreme environments.

In recent years, introducing interstitial oxygen atoms has 
emerged as an effective strengthening strategy, providing a new 
technical approach to improving the fatigue properties of tita- 
nium alloys. The strengthening mechanism of interstitial oxygen 
in titanium alloys, as a typical interstitial solid solution, is char- 
acterized by the dissolution of small-radius oxygen atoms in the 
titanium lattice, preferentially dissolving in the hexagonal close- 
packed (HCP) α-Ti phase [ 10 ]. The embedding of oxygen atoms 
causes signiﬁcant lattice distortion, thereby creating a stress ﬁeld 
around the oxygen atoms. When dislocations move on slip planes, 
these stress ﬁelds must be overcome, requiring greater external 
force to macroscopically improve the yield strength and creep re- 
sistance of the alloy [ 11–13 ]. The strengthening effect, which is 
closely related to the solid solubility of oxygen atoms, becomes 
more signiﬁcant as the oxygen content increases within a cer- 
tain range. However, while excessive oxygen can further enhance 
strength, it promotes the formation of brittle oxides and crack

The fatigue performance of near- α titanium alloys serves as 
a critical indicator of their overall performance and is inﬂuenced 
by a variety of complex factors, including microstructure, envi- 
ronmental medium, temperature, and load spectrum. The fatigue 
failure process of near- α titanium alloys includes crack initiation, 
propagation, and ﬁnal fracture [ 17 , 18 ]. Under the combined ac- 
tion of high temperature and cyclic loading, cracks usually initi- 
ate at stress concentration areas [ 19 ]. Once formed, their propa- 
gation behavior is mainly controlled by the microstructure mor- 
phology of the material (such as α-lamella size, grain boundary 
morphology, silicide precipitation), environmental oxygen erosion, 
and temperature-induced creep-fatigue interaction [ 19 , 20 ]. From 
a macroscopic perspective, crack propagation can be divided into 
three stages: In the ﬁrst stage, crack slowly propagates along a spe- 
ciﬁc crystal plane through shear mechanism; in the second stage, 
crack propagation direction becomes perpendicular to the tensile 
stress direction, and the propagation rate is signiﬁcantly acceler- 
ated, usually conforming to the Paris law; in the third stage, the 
crack rapidly and unstably propagates, leading to fracture. Among 
these, the load frequency, stress ratio ( R -value), and holding time 
(under creep-fatigue loading) are key inﬂuencing factors [ 21–23 ]. It 
is worth noting that at high temperatures, oxygen in the environ- 
ment penetrates along the crack tip, which not only leads to the 
formation of brittle oxides on the metal surface but also acceler- 
ates the local material deterioration. In addition, this phenomenon 
becomes more severe at low frequencies and long stress holding 
times. Therefore, strengthening through thermal exposure by in- 
troducing interstitial oxygen is a double-edged sword, requiring 
meticulous process design to both enhance strength and avoid the 
formation of a continuous brittle oxide layer [ 24 , 25 ]. Ultimately, 
this can synergistically improve the fatigue performance of near- α
titanium alloys, meeting the performance requirements in extreme 
applications. 
As such, this work uses near- α titanium alloy Ti60 (Ti- 
5.8Al-4.0Sn-4.0Zr-1.5Ta-0.4Mo-0.40Si-0.7Nb-0.06C) as an example 
to deeply understand the mechanism by which the oxygen- 
enriched layer (OEL) inﬂuences the selection of crack initiation 
sites and propagation paths through thermal exposure experi- 
ments, and to elucidate the correlation between interstitial oxy- 
gen atoms and crack initiation. This work provides critical exper- 
imental evidence for understanding the interstitial atom strength- 
ening mechanism and offers important theoretical guidance for the 
compositional design and thermal process optimization of next- 
generation high-fatigue-resistance near- α titanium alloys. 
2. Experimental and computational methods 
2.1. Sample fabrication 
Ti60 alloy bars used in this work were provided by the Insti- 
tute of Metal Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Their chemi- 
cal composition is listed in Table 1 . Ti60 alloy bars were machined 
into smooth rod-shaped samples using wire electrical discharge 
machining. The machined rod-shaped samples underwent a 100- 
h thermal exposure experiment at 600 °C, followed by air cooling, 
which was selected based on the service environment of Ti60 al- 
loy. Hereafter, the unexposed samples were named as UE, while 
the thermally exposed samples were named as TE. 
2.2. Mechanical property testing 
High-cycle fatigue (HCF) testing was conducted at room tem- 
perature using an electrohydraulic servo fatigue testing system (K- 
ESF104), with a stress ratio R = 0.1 and a frequency of 10 Hz. 
The tests were terminated when the fatigue life reached 107 cy- 
cles without fracture. The samples were 52 mm in length and 4 
mm in gauge length with a cross-sectional area of 2.5 mm2 . To 
ensure data reliability, the tests were conducted at ﬁve different 
stress levels, with each stress level repeated 2–3 times. The failure 
criterion was deﬁned as complete fracture of the specimen. 
Microhardness testing of the thermally exposed samples was 
performed using an in-situ nanoindenter (Oxford Femto tools, 4- 
Pack FT S20 0 0 0 0 NI) equipped with a Berkovich indenter. The test 
adopted a load-control mode (loading within 10 s, holding at a 
peak load of 50 mN for 10 s, and unloading within 25 s). In-situ 
nanoindentation experiments were conducted at ﬁve distances (ap- 
proximately 5, 20, 40, 60, and 85 μm) from the surface of the ther- 
mally exposed samples. At least three indentations were performed 
at each distance to investigate the mechanism by which thermal 
exposure enhances the mechanical properties of Ti60 alloy. 
Micropillars at three positions (i.e., 5, 15, and 25 μm) were 
extracted from the surface of the thermally exposed alloy using 
a plasma-focused ion beam analyzer (PFIB, Thermo Scientiﬁc He- 
lios 5 PFIB Cxe) and a dual-beam scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) (FEI Helios 5 PFIB DualBeam) for compression testing. Uni- 
axial compression tests were performed on the micropillars using 
an in-situ nanoindenter (Oxford Femto tools, 4-Pack FT S20 0 0 0 0 
NI) equipped with a ﬁt punch tip with a diameter of 20 μm. These 
tests were conducted in displacement-control mode with a nomi- 
nal strain rate of approximately 10−2 s−1 and the maximum strain 
of 20%. 
2.3. Microstructure characterization 
The microstructure and fracture morphology of the samples 
were analyzed using a SOPTOP CX40M optical microscope and a 
SEM (Thermo Fisher, Apreo 2C) equipped with an energy dispersive 
X-ray spectrometer (EDS, Bruker XFlash 7). Phase distribution and 
orientation were obtained using electron back-scattered diffraction 
(EBSD) with backscattered diffraction (Oxford NordlysNano) at an 
accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a step size of 0.1 μm. EBSD 
samples were polished by either electropolishing or in-situ rock- 
ing polishing with PFIB. Thin sheets of deformed micropillars af- 
ter compression testing and a needle-shaped atom probe tomog- 
raphy (APT) sample were fabricated using PFIB. The oxygen distri- 
bution characteristics of Ti60 alloy under thermally exposed con- 
ditions were obtained using APT with a CAMECA LEAP 50 0 0 APT 
scanner. Grain boundaries and surface cracks in the thermally ex- 
posed sample were selectively processed into thin sheets using 
PFIB for transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis. A pro- 
tective W layer was deposited on the micropillars prior to FIB pro- 
cessing. Subsequently, the stripped sheets were thinned to approxi- 
mately 100–150 nm using Xe+ beams. Their morphology and struc- 
tural features were observed using a double aberration-corrected 
TEM (FEI Spectra 200). High-angle annular dark-ﬁeld (HAADF) and 
iDPC-STEM analyses were performed at an accelerating voltage of 
200 kV with a probe convergence angle of 21.5 mrad, HAADF col-

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 1 shows the microstructure of the Ti60 alloy samples before 
and after thermal exposure. Both untreated and thermally exposed 
samples exhibit surface roughness Ra ≤ 0.4 μm, which has a negli- 
gible effect on fatigue behavior. The UE sample exhibits a bimodal 
microstructure ( Fig. 1 (a and b)), mainly composed of equiaxed pri- 
mary α phase ( αP ) and lath-like secondary α phase ( αS ). After 
thermal exposure at 600 °C for 100 h, an OEL with a thickness 
of about 5 μm is formed on the TE sample surface. The internal 
structure is similar to that of the UE sample, still displaying a bi- 
modal microstructure ( Fig. 1 (c and d)). Thermal exposure did not 
alter the microstructure of the Ti60 alloy, only forming an OEL on 
the sample surface, while the bimodal microstructure inside the 
alloy remains unchanged [ 26 ]. 
3.2. Fatigue performance 
In order to compare the effects of thermal exposure on the fa- 
tigue performance of Ti60 alloy, HCF tests were conducted. Fig. 2 
shows the total strain curves and cyclic loops of Ti60 samples at 
650 MPa. The fatigue strain curves of both UE and TE samples can 
be divided into three stages, corresponding to the accumulation 
of plastic deformation caused by the ratcheting effect [ 27 ], plastic 
deformation and fatigue crack propagation in the stage of fatigue 
crack initiation or early damage, and a sharp increase in fatigue 
crack propagation rate followed by fatigue failure. The crack initia- 
tion point is determined by ﬁtting two oblique lines from the ris- 
ing segments of the maximum and minimum strain curves ( Fig. 2 (a 
and c)). Crack initiation or early damage can be identiﬁed by the 
gradual decline of dynamic Young’s modulus with increasing cy- 
cles [ 28 ]. According to the strain accumulation curve, the UE sam- 
ple initiates cracks after 250 0 0 cycles and fractures after 280 0 0 cy- 
cles, while the TE sample initiates cracks earlier (after 70 0 0 cycles) 
and fractures later (after 47500 cycles) ( Fig. 2 (b and d)). Note that 
only 2–3 specimens were tested per stress level in the HCF anal- 
ysis, which may lead to data ﬂuctuations. The total fatigue strain 
is dominated by two factors: cyclic ratcheting-induced strain and 
fatigue damage-induced strain; these two strains are determined 
by the change of the stress-strain curves with the number of fa- 
tigue cycles [ 29 ], and both increase with the increase in the cycle 
number. Fatigue damage begins with early damage, and the cyclic 
ratcheting effect runs throughout the entire fatigue process. 
Based on the stress-strain curves shown in Fig. 2 , the strains 
of the cyclic ratcheting and fatigue damage were measured (as 
shown in Fig. 3 (a)). The results reveal that the early damage cy- 
cle of the UE sample is 3.5 times longer than that of the TE sam- 
ple, whereas its subsequent fatigue damage stage is much shorter. 
Under 650 MPa stress, fatigue damage contributes more than the 
cyclic ratcheting effect to the total fatigue strain in both sample 
groups, indicating that the crack initiation and propagation are the 
main mechanisms of fatigue failure. The TE sample exhibits a gen- 
tler maximum stress versus number of cycles to failure ( S - N ) curve 
slope and superior fatigue strength at 107 cycles ( Fig. 3 (b)). The 
fracture morphology of the UE sample exhibits a rough fracture 
surface, consisting of dimples, cleavage steps, and irregular crack 
propagation paths ( Fig. 3 (c and d)), which suggests that the fa- 
tigue cracks tend to nucleate randomly at local stress concentra- 
tions and rapidly propagate, leading to alloy failure and reduced 
fatigue strength [ 30 , 31 ]. In contrast, the TE sample displays a ﬂat 
and thin layered morphology in the near-edge OEL region, a broad 
river-like layered morphology near the fracture center, and reduced 
morphological ﬂuctuation and dimple density in the inner region 
( Fig. 3 (e and f)). This layered fracture morphology highlights the 
crucial role of thermal exposure in suppressing fatigue crack prop- 
agation. The river-like structure in the fracture morphology also 
suggests that crack propagation may be impeded by microstruc- 
tural barriers (e.g., grain boundaries, phase boundaries, or OELs).

4. Discussion 
4.1. Inhibition of crack propagation by OEL 
To further analyze the inhibitory effect of the OEL on crack 
propagation, the distribution of cracks near the fracture surface of 
the UE and TE samples was further investigated. Only a few ir- 
regular microcracks can be observed near the fracture surface of 
the UE sample, and no obvious long cracks are found ( Fig. 4 (a and 
b)). This conﬁrms that cracks initiate at stress concentration points 
and propagate rapidly, eventually causing fatigue fracture [ 32 ]. For 
the TE sample, a large number of orderly arranged crack initia- 
tion points are observed on the fracture surface. Moreover, it was 
found that the crack propagation direction is deﬂected, forming 
ﬁner branches ( Fig. 4 (c and d)). After fatigue fracture, the micro- 
crack density in the TE samples is signiﬁcantly higher than that 
in the UE samples, while the crack length is signiﬁcantly reduced, 
and these cracks are deﬂected after passing through the OEL. Sta- 
tistical results indicate that plenty of scattered non-fracture-related 
microcracks emerge in TE samples, featuring an average spacing of 
7.2 μm and an average length of 13.8 μm, while only one pene- 
trating crack forms in UE samples. This indicates that the OEL can 
absorb a large amount of energy during fatigue crack initiation and 
further effectively inhibit crack propagation. 
Further analysis was conducted on the differences in crack ini- 
tiation between the TE and UE samples, and their surface charac- 
teristics were analyzed using EBSD. To avoid artiﬁcial interference, 
both surfaces were prepared by PFIB in-situ rocking polishing un- 
der identical conditions ( Fig. 5 (a and d)). Inverse pole ﬁgures (IPFs) 
reveal that near-surface grains in the UE sample present obvious 
orientation scattering, while those in the TE sample remain con- 
sistently oriented ( Fig. 5 (b and e)). Analysis of the local misorien- 
tation distribution using Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) im- 
ages reveals that the near-surface region of the UE sample exhibits 
higher KAM values both within the grains and at grain boundaries 
( Fig. 5 (c)), corresponding to the stress concentrations near the sur- 
face that trigger rapid crack nucleation and propagation. In con- 
trast, high KAM values in the TE sample exist only in the range 
of ∼3 μm from the surface ( Fig. 5 (f)), owing to the formation of 
OEL and the elimination of surface stress concentration. In addi- 
tion, the cracks in the TE sample split and branch after propagat- 
ing ∼4 μm inward, accompanied by signiﬁcant orientation inho- 
mogeneity around the crack tip ( Fig. 5 (g and h)). Due to the hin- 
dering effect of the OEL, crack propagation is inhibited, and signif- 
icant stress concentration is induced at the crack tip. This causes 
crystal orientation deﬂection, and the crack branching process con- 
sumes the propagation energy [ 33 ]. In order to understand the hin- 
dering mechanism of the OEL on crack propagation, the surface 
cracks of the TE sample were processed into thin sheets by PFIB for 
TEM analysis. Bright-ﬁeld TEM image ( Fig. 5 (i)) and EDS analysis 
( Fig. 5 (j)) reveal that the cracks stop propagating after multiple bi- 
furcations, and oxygen enrichment and titanium depletion are ob- 
served at the crack tip, further conﬁrming the inhibitory effect of

4.2. Formation mechanism of OEL 
The introduction of OEL into Ti60 alloy can signiﬁcantly en- 
hance its fatigue resistance. In order to elucidate the strengthen- 
ing mechanism of the OEL, further investigations were conducted 
on its formation mechanism and mechanical properties. Fig. 6 (a) 
quantiﬁes the concentration (in at.%) of Ti and O in APT specimens 
of Ti60 alloy within 0–20 μm depth from the surface. For the un- 
exposed Ti60 alloy, the 3D reconstruction results of APT reveal that 
a small amount of oxygen atoms (4–6 at.%) are relatively uniformly 
dispersed in the α-Ti phase, while the Ti content stabilizes at 80–
85 at.% owing to its dominant matrix role. After thermal exposure, 
the oxygen content increases signiﬁcantly and mainly segregates 
near grain boundaries, with a maximum content of 15 at.%, accom- 
panied by a slight drop of Ti content within the 70–85 at.% range, 
which is attributed to oxygen substitution at partial Ti lattice sites. 
Such a segregation feature proves that grain boundaries serve as 
the predominant diffusion pathways for oxygen during thermal ex- 
posure [ 36 ]. Further TEM characterization on near-surface grain 
boundaries of the TE sample reveals obvious lattice distortion near 
α/ β interfaces ( Fig. 6 (b–d)). Meanwhile, inverse Fast Fourier Trans- 
formation (FFT) analysis conﬁrms the presence of dislocations near 
the interface ( Fig. 6 (e)), and the GPA graph clearly shows the local 
stress ﬁeld at this location ( Fig. 6 (f)). These observations indicate 
that oxygen atoms inﬁltrate the alloy along grain boundaries and 
occupy interstitial positions, thereby altering the crystal structure 
and generating local stress ﬁelds [ 37 ]. 
The formation mechanism of the OEL is summarized by ana- 
lyzing the fracture microstructure, crystal structure characteristics, 
and simulation results at the grain boundaries of the TE sample. 
During thermal exposure, oxygen atoms are ﬁrst adsorbed onto the 
alloy surface. Owing to the lower diffusion activation energy at the 
interfaces than inside the lattice, oxygen atoms diffuse much faster

4.3. Mechanical properties of OEL 
To verify the strengthening effect of the OEL, nanoindentation 
and micropillar compression were employed to characterize the lo- 
cal mechanical properties of the TE sample. In-situ nanoindenta- 
tion experiments were conducted at ﬁve distances (approximately 
5, 20, 40, 60, and 85 μm) from the surface. As shown in Fig. 7 (a), 
the indentation size increases with increasing depth. The load- 
displacement curves ( Fig. 7 (b)) indicate that both the maximum 
and residual indentation depths rise gradually with distance from 
the surface, and the values at 5 μm are considerably lower than 
those at other locations. This suggests that oxygen doping signif- 
icantly improves the hardness by inducing lattice distortion via 
interstitial penetration into grain boundaries and the matrix. The 
depths from 20 to 85 μm correspond to the matrix region be- 
yond the OEL, where the mechanical properties are notably lower 
without oxygen strengthening. The nanohardness ( Hv ) distribution 
( Fig. 7 (c)) exhibits a decreasing trend with increasing depth, which 
is directly attributed to the gradient oxygen content in the surface 
layer. 
To analyze the local mechanical properties of the OEL, micropil- 
lar compressive tests were performed at distances of 5 μm (Mi- 
cropillar 1), 15 μm (Micropillar 2), and 25 μm (Micropillar 3) from 
the surface, selecting grains with < 11−20 > orientation favoring 
< a > slip [ 40 ] via EBSD ( Fig. 8 (b)). As the engineering compres- 
sive strain increases, a sequential evolution of deformation behav- 
ior and its corresponding mechanical responses was observed in 
Micropillar 1, reﬂecting the inﬂuence of oxygen doping on its de- 
formation mechanism. The ﬁrst slip trace emerges at ε ≈ 0.04, cor- 
responding to a yield strength of 1701 ± 8 MPa ( Fig. 8 (c and d1)). 
The curve enters a plateau period at ε ≈ 0.08 ( Fig. 8 (c and d2)), in- 
dicating that intragranular slip occurs along the pre-activated slip 
plane [ 41 ]. At ε ≈ 0.1, the number of slip traces on one side of 
the initial slip band increases, while the ﬁrst slip trace appears on 
the other side, causing a slight upward ﬂuctuation in the stress- 
strain curve ( Fig. 8 (c and d3)), indicating activation of multiple 
slip planes with mutual shear [ 42 , 43 ]. With increasing strain, addi- 
tional slip traces form gradually and distribute symmetrically at ε
≈ 0.18 ( Fig. 8 (d4)), accompanied by a maximum strength of 2574 ±
10 MPa. The homogeneous and coordinated deformation behavior 
indicates that oxygen doping in the OEL suppresses localized shear 
and promotes uniform deformation, thus signiﬁcantly enhancing 
strength [ 44 ]. 
Compared with Micropillar 1, Micropillar 2 exhibits different 
deformation behavior and mechanical response ( Fig. 8 (e1–e4)). 
Micropillar 2 yields at a lower strain ( ε ≈ 0.03) with a lower 
yield strength of 1290 ± 5 MPa. With increasing strain, multi- 
ple slip planes are activated, local deformation occurs along the 
pre-activated slip plane [ 45 ], and the strength decreases signiﬁ- 
cantly. The maximum strength reaches 2252 ± 7 MPa at ε ≈ 0.18, 
lower than that of Micropillar 1 due to the reduced oxygen content 
and weaker deformation constraint. Consequently, local deforma- 
tion occurs on a few slip planes, resulting in an overall strength. 
For Micropillar 3, which is farthest from the alloy surface, the 
ﬁrst slip trace appears much earlier, only at ε ≈ 0.015, and yield 
strength drops to 894 ± 9 MPa ( Fig. 8 (c and f1)). With increas- 
ing strain, it exhibits severe localized deformation, rapid forma- 
tion of dense slip steps, and large strength ﬂuctuations( Fig. 8 (f2–
f4)). The maximum strength is only 1797 ± 12 MPa. The low oxy- 
gen content provided the weakest resistance to dislocation motion, 
leading to pronounced localized shear and low strength. Although 
the strain rate used in micropillar compression is relatively high, it 
may slightly increase the yield strength, but it does not change the 
decreasing trend with distance from the surface.

4.4. Fatigue fracture mechanisms of thermally exposed Ti60 alloy 
Based on the above results, introducing OEL into Ti60 alloy can 
effectively suppress crack propagation, alter fatigue fracture be- 
havior, and signiﬁcantly enhance its fatigue resistance. The fatigue 
fracture mechanisms of unexposed and thermally exposed Ti60 al- 
loy are summarized as follows. 
Unexposed Ti60 alloy is prone to microcracks on the sample 
surface, accompanied by local plastic deformation. These micro- 
cracks propagate along relatively stable grain boundaries or slip 
systems. In the continuous loading cycle, some microcracks fur- 
ther propagate and coalesce, gradually evolving into macrocracks 
and causing early damage [ 50 ]. At the same time, the effective

5. Conclusions 
This study investigates the mechanism by which thermal expo- 
sure enhances the fatigue properties of Ti60 alloy, focusing on the 
inhibitory role of the OEL on crack propagation. Thermal exposure 
formed an OEL on the sample surface while retaining the bimodal 
microstructure, which effectively relieved surface stress concentra- 
tion and enabled the alloy to exhibit excellent fatigue strength at 
650 MPa up to 107 cycles. Multiscale characterization conﬁrmed 
that the OEL promoted the formation of massive dispersed micro- 
cracks instead of a single dominant crack, signiﬁcantly enhancing 
energy dissipation and suppressing unstable crack growth. Atomic- 
scale characterizations demonstrated that oxygen atoms mainly 
existed in interstitial form and segregated along grain bound- 
aries as rapid diffusion paths, and the OSFs formed at crack tips 
could absorb energy, deﬂect crack propagation paths, and alleviate 
local stress concentration. In-situ nanoindentation and micropil- 
lar compression experiments also conﬁrm the excellent mechani- 
cal properties of the OEL. These ﬁndings provide a feasible engi- 
neering strategy for enhancing the fatigue performance of high- 
temperature titanium alloys in hot-end structural applications.
